Independent manufacturing project

Can higher sales support profit and cash?

I’m a Mechanical Engineering Technology student at CUNY City Tech, interested in engineering and financial analysis.

I built a 12-month Excel model for financial planning and analysis (FP&A), connecting sales, production costs and staffing to profit and cash flow. It includes working capital: money tied up in daily operations.

Why it matters

Positive earnings do not guarantee enough cash.

The model earns $226,663 before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. But more money is needed for customer receivables and inventory, net of supplier credit, as well as equipment.

How I built it

One set of operating inputs, 12 linked worksheets.

I linked the monthly sales plan to production costs and support staffing, then followed the effects through working capital, equipment spending and cash. The model compares the original budget with an updated outlook.

Build a connected plan

Units and prices drive revenue. Unit costs, hiring and pay assumptions drive expenses. Cross-sheet formulas keep the budget, forecast and cash schedules connected.

Explain what changed

Variance analysis compares results with the budget. I separated the effects of volume, price, material costs, conversion costs and payroll to explain the earnings gap.

Test the model

I tested lower volume and higher material costs, then used 12 reconciliation checks to compare linked calculations. All show OK in the saved workbook.

Excel techniques: cross-sheet and mixed cell references, monthly rollforwards, SUM, AVERAGE, IF, ABS, ROUND and SUMPRODUCT. See formulas, checks and model scope.

Conclusion & learning

Review costs and cash alongside sales.

For this fictional manufacturer, higher revenue is not enough to protect earnings or fund investment. I would investigate material costs, support hiring, customer collections and equipment timing together.

What I learned

A total tells me what happened; a variance bridge helps explain why. Separating profit from cash also makes the funding needs clearer.

I learned to keep inputs easy to find and to check that related schedules agree. That makes the model easier to trace and discuss.

What this model cannot tell us

  • The company and data are fictional. The results demonstrate analysis, not a real business outcome.
  • One product group, 30-day months and simplified cost and working-capital assumptions limit the detail.
  • The cash forecast excludes tax, interest and financing. There is no depreciation or net-income schedule.

Opening working-capital balances contribute to the large January cash drop and would need validation for a real business. Reconciliation checks establish consistency, not realistic assumptions.

Assumptions and forecast inputs

The business case

Northstar Components represents a manufacturer with one product group. The project tests whether its sales plan can cover production costs, support staff and other operating expenses, while leaving enough cash for equipment and day-to-day operations.

I used a manufacturing setting because units, material cost and production cost give the financial model a clear operating basis. The aim is to explain why the forecast changes, rather than only report a final profit number.

Reading the bridge

The $39,632 selling-price benefit is almost fully offset by the $39,667 increase from material unit costs. Conversion costs and support payroll add further pressure.

This is why I would review material costs and hiring alongside sales. Reporting revenue alone would miss the deterioration in operating profit.

The SG&A line contains its full variance, including variable selling expenses. The price line is a revenue effect before those expenses.

Cash calculation and monthly balances

Positive EBITDA, negative cash flow

The outlook produces $226,663 of EBITDA, but $536,946 of additional net working capital and $369,000 of capital expenditure (CapEx), or equipment spending, use more cash than the operation generates.

$226,663 − $536,946 − $369,000 = −$679,283EBITDA − increase in net working capital − CapEx = cash flow before tax and financing

Opening cash of $750,000 plus cash flow of −$679,283 leaves $70,717 at year-end.

Excel formulas, reconciliation checks, and model limits

How the worksheets connect

Assumptions and Actuals feed Budget, Forecast and Headcount. Working capital and CapEx then feed Cash flow. Dashboard, Variance and Scenario summarize the results. Checks reviews the calculations without feeding operating formulas.

Revenue

Budget!F10 = F8*F9

January: 4,150 units × $135 = $560,250.

Cost of goods sold

Budget!F16 = SUM(F14:F15)

Materials ($186,750) + conversion costs ($103,750) = $290,500. Direct production labor is already included in conversion cost.

Support payroll

Headcount!F16 = F13*F14/'Assumptions'!$D$35*(1+F15)

Average FTE × annual salary ÷ 12 × (1 + burden). January: 20 × $84,000 ÷ 12 × 1.20 = $168,000. Averaging opening and closing FTE approximates mid-month hiring.

EBITDA

Budget!F27 = F17-F21-F25

Gross profit − support payroll − SG&A. CapEx is a cash outflow, not an EBITDA expense.

Working capital

'Working capital'!F15 = F8*F11/'Assumptions'!$D$34

Receivables = monthly revenue × receivable days ÷ 30. Inventory and payables use COGS as a simplified run rate. Net working capital = receivables + inventory − payables.

Cash rollforward

'Cash flow'!F29 = F28+F26

Opening cash + cash flow = closing cash. The next month starts with the previous month’s closing balance.

Techniques demonstrated

Cross-sheet references, absolute and mixed cell references, monthly rollforwards, SUM, AVERAGE, IF, ABS, ROUND and SUMPRODUCT. Annual margins divide annual profit by annual revenue, rather than averaging monthly percentages. Ending balances use December rather than the sum of 12 months.

The workbook has 12 reconciliation checks covering revenue, EBITDA, payroll, working capital, cash and the scenario. All show OK in the reviewed copy. These confirm internal consistency; they do not establish that the assumptions are realistic.

Model scope and limitations

  • One product group and 30-day planning months.
  • Conversion costs are modeled per unit; this simplifies production cost behavior.
  • Inventory and supplier payables use COGS-based estimates rather than a purchasing ledger.
  • Payroll and SG&A are paid in the month incurred.
  • No tax, interest, financing, depreciation or net-income schedule. Cash flow is before tax and financing.
