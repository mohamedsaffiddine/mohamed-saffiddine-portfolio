Manufacturing FP&A in Excel

I’m Mohamed Saffiddine, a Mechanical Engineering Technology student at CUNY City Tech interested in engineering and financial analysis.

I built a 12-month FP&A (financial planning and analysis) model linking sales, production costs, staffing and cash flow, including money tied up in operations (working capital).

My question: Do higher sales lead to stronger profit and enough cash to support operations?

01 / What happened to cash?

Positive earnings still use up most of the opening cash.

The model generates $226,663 of EBITDA, but an extra $536,946 goes into working capital and $369,000 into equipment. Together, these leave a cash outflow of $679,283 before tax and financing.

The large January outflow partly comes from low opening working-capital balances. I would validate those balances before using this model for a real cash decision.

Source: Assumptions!D30; Dashboard!F9:F14; Cash flow!D26:D29. Equipment spending is capital expenditure (CapEx).

02 / How I built it

Connect operating assumptions to profit and cash.

I used 12 linked worksheets to turn monthly sales volumes, prices and unit costs into a budget and updated forecast. Staffing drives support payroll; customer collection, inventory and supplier payment assumptions feed cash planning.

Budgeting & forecasting

Build a monthly plan, then combine six months of simulated actuals with a six-month forecast.

Variance & scenario analysis

Explain changes in earnings and test how lower volume and higher material costs affect the plan.

Excel modeling & checks

Use linked formulas, monthly rollforwards and 12 reconciliation checks to trace results.

Model path: Assumptions / Actuals → Budget / Forecast / Headcount → Working capital / CapEx → Cash flow. Dashboard, Variance and Scenario summarize the results.

03 / Conclusion & learning

I would review costs and cash alongside sales.

Higher material and production costs leave little extra gross profit, while support payroll rises. Before supporting further spending, I would investigate pricing, the need for additional hiring, customer collections and equipment timing.

What I learned: A useful forecast explains why results change. Separating inputs from formulas makes the model easier to follow, and comparing earnings with cash shows demands that a sales total misses.

Main limitations: This is a simplified learning case with one product group and 30-day planning months. It has no tax, interest, financing, depreciation or net-income schedule. The cash figures are before tax and financing; the 12 checks confirm internal consistency, not realistic assumptions.

Reading the bridge

The $39,632 selling-price benefit is almost fully offset by the $39,667 increase from material unit costs. Conversion costs and support payroll add further pressure.

This is why I would review material costs and hiring alongside sales. Reporting revenue alone would miss the deterioration in operating profit.

The SG&A line contains its full variance, including variable selling expenses. The price line is a revenue effect before those expenses.

Scenario and sensitivity: lower volume, higher material costs

What if materials cost 8% more and volume falls 5%?

The Scenario sheet applies this stress to the original annual budget. Selling price, support payroll and fixed SG&A remain unchanged. Conversion cost and variable SG&A decrease with activity.

EBITDA falls by $328,700. Material spending still rises by $59,775: the combination of 5% lower volume and 8% higher unit cost is 0.95 × 1.08 = 1.026, or a 2.6% increase in material spending.

The workbook also includes a sensitivity grid for volume changes from −10% to +5% and material-cost changes from 0% to +12%. At −5% volume and +12% material cost, annual EBITDA becomes negative at −$67,214.

Excel formulas, worksheet links and model checks

Revenue

Budget!F10 = F8*F9

January: 4,150 units × $135 = $560,250.

Cost of goods sold

Budget!F16 = SUM(F14:F15)

Materials ($186,750) + conversion costs ($103,750) = $290,500. Direct production labor is already included in conversion cost.

Support payroll

Headcount!F16 = F13*F14/'Assumptions'!$D$35*(1+F15)

Average FTE × annual salary ÷ 12 × (1 + burden). January: 20 × $84,000 ÷ 12 × 1.20 = $168,000. Averaging opening and closing FTE approximates mid-month hiring.

EBITDA

Budget!F27 = F17-F21-F25

Gross profit − support payroll − SG&A. CapEx is a cash outflow, not an EBITDA expense.

Working capital

'Working capital'!F15 = F8*F11/'Assumptions'!$D$34

Receivables = monthly revenue × receivable days ÷ 30. Inventory and payables use COGS as a simplified run rate. Net working capital = receivables + inventory − payables.

Cash rollforward

'Cash flow'!F29 = F28+F26

Opening cash + cash flow = closing cash. The next month starts with the previous month’s closing balance.

Techniques demonstrated

Cross-sheet references, absolute and mixed cell references, monthly rollforwards, SUM, AVERAGE, IF, ABS, ROUND and SUMPRODUCT. Annual margins divide annual profit by annual revenue, rather than averaging monthly percentages. Ending balances use December rather than the sum of 12 months.

The workbook has 12 reconciliation checks covering revenue, EBITDA, payroll, working capital, cash and the scenario. All show OK in the reviewed copy. These confirm internal consistency; they do not establish that the assumptions are realistic.

Detailed modeling limitations
  • One product group and 30-day planning months.
  • Conversion costs include direct production labor and variable factory costs; per-unit modeling simplifies production cost behavior.
  • Inventory and supplier payables use cost of goods sold (COGS) estimates rather than a purchasing ledger.
  • Payroll and selling, general and administrative expenses (SG&A) are paid in the month incurred.
  • No tax, interest, financing, depreciation or net-income schedule. Cash flow is before tax and financing.
  • Opening working-capital balances need validation before a real cash decision.
  • Simulated company data, with no client or employer relationship. No savings or business impact are claimed.
